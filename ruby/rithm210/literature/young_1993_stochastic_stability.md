# P3-R9 Literature Note A — Young (1993), Conventions and Stochastic Stability

**Primary file:** [Young, The Evolution of Conventions, Econometrica 61(1), 57–84](https://drive.google.com/file/d/11EVvAWI3fMzFtKi-qqOcI3dR8gBVAN73/view). Original PDF title, pages, author verified before renaming/moving.

## What the original paper establishes
The repeated n-person game involves individuals drawn from a larger finite population rather than necessarily the same six participants throughout. Each chosen strategy uses a sample of others' previous actions. Theorem 1: for a **weakly acyclic** n-person game and a sufficient restriction on sample size k relative to memory length m and best-reply-path length, the adaptive process converges almost surely to a convention. Theorem 2: with rare experimentation, the stochastically stable recurrent classes minimize stochastic potential, obtained through resistance-weighted paths between recurrent classes. In generic 2x2 coordination games, the selection connects to risk dominance (Theorem 3). These are nontrivial mathematical results, not intuitive slogans.

## Boundary against RITHM
The actual RITHM experiment has **stable six-person groups** and 40 experimental periods; Young's randomly rematched population and infinitesimal experimentation limits do not automatically apply. 'Convergence to some convention' is distinct from predicting the observed 154 early vs 257 late transitions CONDITIONAL on n_A=4, including P(no actual switches), gross activity and welfare. One cannot conclude Young's stochastic stability forces short-horizon n=4 persistence without verifying the full Markov state (strategy histories, memory, population assignment) and the stated hypotheses.

## Testable research connection
Build two models with identical payoff table and sample rule, but different encounter schedules: fixed six actors versus randomly rematched population. Their convention-selection and within-state activity hazards need not agree. The preexisting Young result is the strong benchmark; any contribution must offer a finite-time bound or intervention prediction for the observed group cost, not merely 'conventions emerge'.
