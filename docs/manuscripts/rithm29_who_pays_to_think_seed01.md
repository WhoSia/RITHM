# RITHM-2.9 — Manuscript Seed 01 / WORKING DRAFT

**Provisional title:** *Who Pays to Think? Computation Costs, Action Memory, and Equilibrium Selection in Finite Resource Games*

**Status:** P0–P1 working manuscript; **not submitted / not a theorem promotion**. Date 2026-10-19. The claim is about a *contracted class* of programs, not general intelligence or human cognition.

## Abstract (draft v0.1)
An agent who chooses a resource may also choose whether to compute before acting. That choice is often modeled as costless information availability, but calculation itself can change both private incentives and collective congestion. We construct a two-agent finite resource game in which each participant can execute a charged READ–READ–XOR–ACT program or take a default action without observing the hidden state. The program changes the chosen resource, so even a correct computation may impose congestion costs on others. In the one-period game, pure equilibria assign computation to exactly one agent, while a symmetric mixed equilibrium has each agent compute with probability one-half. We then introduce public memory of the previous action, physical switching costs, and a finite horizon, and compute backward pure Markov-equilibrium correspondences among the declared programs. The resulting exercise separates three quantities that are easily conflated: the value of an observation, the expenditure on a computation, and the transition cost of carrying the computed choice into action. The framework is intentionally restricted. It offers a reproducible basis for testing claims about equilibrium selection under computational friction; it does not establish a universal complexity bound or identify actual human cognitive costs.

## Introduction (working prose)
A route-choice model may correctly predict which road a traveler takes without explaining what the traveler computed before choosing it. Sometimes the traveler follows yesterday's path because changing it is expensive. Sometimes information is available but searching for it is costly. Sometimes the traveler has the information yet does not finish the calculations that would make it useful. These are different mechanisms. If a model represents all three with one inertial penalty, even an accurate behavioral fit may not say which intervention would help.

Our question is narrower and more demanding. Suppose that the acts of reading, calculating, and changing one's selected resource are individually priced. The outcome of a calculation changes the load experienced by other users. When, if ever, do strategic agents choose complementary levels of computation, and how does yesterday's action alter that choice? We start with a deliberately small game because a proposed mechanism should survive exact payoff enumeration before a large simulation makes it difficult to audit.

Prior work rules out an easy novelty claim. Simon (1955), Russell and Wefald (1991), Lipman (1991), and Lieder and Griffiths (2019) already establish important conceptual structures for bounded reasoning and meta-level choice. Castiglioni et al. (2021) and Griesbach et al. (2024) study information design in congestion games, while Zeng et al. (SSRN preprint) explicitly combines information pricing and congestion. These sources are **DRIVE HELD** in the research corpus. Our distinction is not simply that cognition or information has a price: it is the attempt to make an executable, resource-bounded computation program part of a recurrent strategic game. Whether this yields a new nontrivial theorem remains open.

## Formal model and audit boundary
The declared instruction alphabet is STOP or READ X; READ Y; XOR; ACT. A fresh environmental world contains two independent fair bits; their parity identifies the correct resource. A computation costs 2r+c and determines the selected resource. STOP chooses default resource zero. Each individual incurs load equal to the number using their resource, a penalty for choosing a resource different from parity, the cost of executed operations, and—in the sequential extension—a fee when their choice differs from their previous public physical action. The state `(remaining horizon, last actions of all agents)` is sufficient only under the specified iid world and public-action assumptions. In every subgame, a policy is chosen among exact best-response profiles; when several equilibria exist the computation explicitly reports their number and uses a disclosed tie-breaking selector. We do not infer that people use this selector.

## Red-team referee note (v0.1)
**Most serious criticism:** the chosen READ–READ–XOR task embeds a price by definition and allows only two whole programs. The apparent strategic allocation of computation might simply be ordinary 2x2 anti-coordination under a different label. Prove an invariant across a nontrivial family of instruction languages or weaken the claim to a transparent benchmark. **Experiment changed as a result:** add dynamic full subgame-Nash checking with public previous actions and an independent Go oracle before discussing publishability.
